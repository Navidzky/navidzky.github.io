% ======================= JOB MARKET PAPER =======================
\section{Job Market Paper}

\paper{The Economics of Chokepoints: Remote Matching and Entry Deterrence in Global Oil Tanker Markets}
Maritime chokepoints are conventionally modeled as raising only the marginal cost of transport.
I argue they also act as an entry cost: because tankers are fixed remotely and days in advance,
carriers choose which distant market to commit to under uncertainty about conditions on arrival,
so an anticipated chokepoint restriction deters entry before any physical closure occurs --- a
channel that local, random-matching frameworks cannot generate. I distinguish gateways, whose
closure disconnects a trading region (e.g., the Strait of Hormuz), from passages with viable
substitutes (e.g., Bab el-Mandeb): gateway disruptions shift the fleet sharply and the shift
persists after reopening, while passage disruptions produce gradual rerouting. Carriers withdraw
from gateway-affected markets strictly before closure, and freight rates understate the cost of
disruption because cargoes that attract no applicants leave no price record. The 2026 Strait of
Hormuz restriction supplies the identifying episode.

% ======================= WORKING PAPERS =======================
\section{Working Papers}

\paper{When Outside Options Shape the Labor Market: A Directed Search Perspective}
\begin{desc}
  \item Studies how differences in outside options across workers affect wages, firm entry, and job
  allocation. I develop a directed search model with skilled and unskilled workers and high- and
  low-tech firms, where only skilled workers hold outside options. Stronger outside options raise
  posted wages for skilled workers but can reduce high-tech firm entry; as fewer high-tech jobs are
  created, skilled workers' expected wages may fall, and unskilled workers are affected indirectly
  through changes in firm composition and wage competition.
\end{desc}

\paper{\href{https://dx.doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4830956}{The Heterogeneous Effects of Exogenous Shocks on Public Transportation Demand}}
\begin{desc}
  \item With Kowsar Yousefi and Amir Samimi.
  \item A sudden gasoline price shock increased subway usage by roughly 3.9 percent, implying a
  cross-price elasticity of about 0.044, while subway usage fell by roughly 42.6 percent during
  the COVID-19 pandemic.
\end{desc}
